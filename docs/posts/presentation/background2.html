##Maryland’s typically a young defense. But since Locksley’s took over, there’s been a drastic shift over year-to-year of wherether the Terps are playing freshman or not. They’ve played a ton in some years (2019,2021 and 2025) and barely played some in other years. I think this just shows the overall instablity during the Locksley tenure. There’s just a lack of cohesion and overall plan on how many freshman they’re going to play. They’ve tried to be more veteran heavy in recent years, as you can see, but then this season, they relied on a ton of freshman this year. Sidney Stewart, Zahir Mathis and Messiah Dellhome all played signifcant snaps and were expected to be the best players on the defense.

##This regression lines show that while Maryland’s freshman usage on defense changes dramatically year to year, the data shows no strong relationship between how many freshman snaps are played and defensive performance measured by points allowed per game. In fact, if there is any sort of relationship, it’s actually that if you play more freshman, there defense has performed better. This shows that Maryland’s issue isn’t simply playing young players—it’s inconsistency in the program’s roster quality and development.

# Years and corresponding opponents
years <- 2015:2025
opponents <- c(
  "bostoncollege",  # 2015
  "alabama",        # 2016
  "alabama",        # 2017
  "missst",         # 2018
  "osu",            # 2019
  "marshall",       # 2020
  "wisconsin",      # 2021
  "airforce",       # 2022
  "michigan",       # 2023
  "indiana",        # 2024
  "osu"             # 2025
)

summary_list <- list()

for (i in seq_along(years)) {
  y   <- years[i]
  opp <- opponents[i]
  
  md_fresh  <- sum(get(paste0("md", y))$snap_counts_defense,  na.rm = TRUE)
  opp_fresh <- sum(get(paste0(opp, y))$snap_counts_defense, na.rm = TRUE)
  
  summary_list[[i]] <- tibble(
    year = y,
    pct_diff = 100 * (md_fresh - opp_fresh) / opp_fresh
  )
}

summary <- bind_rows(summary_list)

ggplot(summary, aes(x = year, y = pct_diff, fill = pct_diff > 0)) +
  geom_col(width = 0.7) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linewidth = 0.5) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("TRUE" = "#C8102E", "FALSE" = "#4B9CD3")) +
  scale_y_continuous(
    limits = c(-200, 200),
    breaks = seq(-200, 200, 50),
    labels = function(x) paste0(x, "%"),
    oob = scales::squish
  ) +
  scale_x_continuous(breaks = 2015:2025) +
  labs(
    title = "Maryland football isn't following the model for elite defenses. Experience",
    subtitle = "Percent difference in freshman defensive snaps (values capped for readability)",
    x = "Season",
    y = "Percent difference (%)"
  ) +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 13) +
  theme(panel.grid.minor = element_blank(), legend.position = "none")

##Look at that. Maryland has played more freshman than the top defense in America seven of the past 10 years. And four of those seven years, there’s been an astronomical difference in how much more freshman the Terps are using than the nation’s best defense. I used help from AI to try and figure out how to scale my chart to show the astronomical difference in the amount of freshman MD is playing compared to some of the nation’s top defenses, because of how large the difference is. If we draw back to those years, they actually did solid in terms of points allowd per game, so the main conclusion I have from this is how poor the Terps are at developing. They bring in some talented freshman (Jaishawn Barham, Chop Robinson, Sydney Stewart, Daniel Wingate and others), but the lack of development those players show over thier time in College Park is a signficant issue. That falls on Locksley and his coaching staff.

##But if Maryland wants to reach the level that its trying to achieve, it needs to either bring in more talented freshman, or get older. The Terps, compared to the No. 1 defense in the nation over the years, played more freshman than that unit seven times since 2015. While some of these teams are substantially better than the Terps have been historically, it’s a good barometer to look too when considering how Maryland’s defense can get better. A number of those units, including Michigan, Ohio State and Indiana all came from the Big Ten.
